🇬🇧 UK University Scholarships Through UCAS

A beginner-friendly guide for Iraqi students applying to undergraduate universities in the United Kingdom.

Q1: What is UCAS?

UCAS stands for the Universities and Colleges Admissions Service. It is the main system used to apply for undergraduate courses at UK universities.

For 2027 entry, one UCAS application allows a student to select up to five university or course choices.

UCAS normally does not award the scholarship. Scholarships are usually provided by:

  • The university itself
  • A university department
  • The UK government
  • A charity or external organisation
  • A student’s national government or sponsor

Q2: Can an Iraqi student use UCAS?

Yes. Iraqi students can apply through UCAS as international applicants.

However, every university decides which Iraqi qualifications it accepts. Having an Iraqi secondary certificate does not guarantee direct entry into the first year of every UK university.

Some universities may require:

  • An International Foundation Year
  • A-level qualifications
  • The International Baccalaureate Diploma
  • Another recognised international qualification
  • Evidence of English-language ability

For example, the University of Exeter currently states that the Iraqi National High School Diploma is not sufficient for direct undergraduate entry at Exeter. A recognised foundation programme may be required. Other universities can have different rules.

Q3: What types of scholarships are available?

  1. Automatic scholarships: the university assesses the student automatically after receiving the course application.
  2. Competitive merit scholarships: based on excellent grades, activities, achievements and the quality of the application.
  3. Need-based scholarships: based partly or entirely on the student’s financial circumstances.
  4. Country or regional scholarships: available only to applicants from certain countries or regions, such as Iraq or the Middle East.
  5. Subject scholarships: available only for particular courses such as engineering, science, business, music or sport.
  6. Tuition-fee discounts: reduce the amount paid to the university but do not provide living expenses.
  7. Full scholarships: may cover all tuition and sometimes living costs. These are uncommon and extremely competitive at undergraduate level.

How should an Iraqi student apply?

  1. Decide which subject you want to study.
  2. Search for suitable courses using the official UCAS course search.
  3. Check each university’s entry requirements specifically for Iraqi qualifications.
  4. Check whether a Foundation Year is required.
  5. Check English-language requirements.
  6. Calculate the complete tuition and living costs.
  7. Search both the UCAS scholarship tool and the university’s own scholarship page.
  8. Create the UCAS application and add up to five choices.
  9. Complete the education history and obtain a reference.
  10. Write the three UCAS personal-statement answers honestly and in your own voice.
  11. Submit before the correct course deadline.
  12. After receiving an offer, complete every separate scholarship application.
  13. Keep checking email, including the spam folder.

Documents commonly requested

Requirements vary, but students may need:

  • Passport or valid travel document
  • Official secondary-school certificates
  • Grade transcripts
  • Certified English translations
  • Predicted grades if still studying
  • An academic reference
  • English-language test results when required
  • UCAS personal-statement answers
  • Scholarship essays or additional application forms
  • Evidence of family income for need-based scholarships

The university decides exactly which documents and translations are acceptable.

⚠️ A partial scholarship is not full funding

A scholarship described as “£5,000” may only subtract £5,000 from the tuition fee. It may not cover:

  • The remaining tuition fee
  • Accommodation
  • Food and daily expenses
  • Flights
  • Visa costs
  • Health surcharge
  • Books and equipment

Always calculate the amount still unpaid after the scholarship.

Student visa financial requirement

A scholarship does not automatically remove the UK Student visa financial requirement.

Under the current published rules, a student may need to prove they can pay the unpaid course fees shown on their Confirmation of Acceptance for Studies, plus:

  • £1,529 per month for up to nine months when studying in London.
  • £1,171 per month for up to nine months when studying outside London.

Personal or parental funds normally have to remain in an acceptable account for at least 28 consecutive days. Evidence must meet the current visa rules.

These amounts can change. Always check the official UK Government website before applying for a visa.

What about postgraduate scholarships?

Chevening is available to eligible applicants from Iraq, but it is for eligible one-year taught master’s degrees—not undergraduate study.

Chevening is applied for through its own application system, not through the undergraduate UCAS application. Its current application cycle is closed.

Iraq is not included in the British Council’s published list of countries eligible for GREAT Scholarships in the 2026–27 academic year. Students should check again in future years because the country list can change.

🚨 Avoid scholarship scams

  • Do not pay anyone who guarantees a scholarship.
  • Check that the address belongs to UCAS, GOV.UK, Chevening or the university itself.
  • Do not send passport copies or bank documents to unknown social-media accounts.
  • Never publish personal documents on a public website.
  • Confirm every scholarship on the university’s official website before applying.

1. Where should a complete beginner start?

Your first target: A1 English

English ability is commonly divided into six CEFR levels:

  1. A1: Beginner
  2. A2: Elementary
  3. B1: Intermediate
  4. B2: Upper-intermediate
  5. C1: Advanced
  6. C2: Highly proficient

At the end of A1, a student should be able to:

  • Introduce themselves
  • Say where they live and study
  • Talk simply about family and daily life
  • Ask and answer basic questions
  • Understand slow, clear everyday speech
  • Read short messages and signs
  • Write short sentences and simple messages
Important: A 12-week plan is not a promise of fluency. Progress depends on study time, practice, previous knowledge and consistency.
2. The five rules for learning English
  1. Practise regularly: 30 minutes every day is generally better than one very long lesson each week.
  2. Learn complete phrases: learn “How are you?” rather than memorising only the separate words.
  3. Use the language: listening and reading are not enough. Speak and write too.
  4. Review repeatedly: revisit vocabulary after one day, three days, one week and two weeks.
  5. Accept mistakes: mistakes show what needs more practice.
Do not only learn: “breakfast”
Learn a useful sentence: “I eat breakfast at seven o’clock.”

6. How to improve listening

Use the three-listen method

  1. First listen: listen without reading. Try to understand the main idea.
  2. Second listen: listen while reading the transcript or subtitles.
  3. Third listen: pause after every sentence and repeat it aloud.

Choose short recordings that:

  • Use beginner vocabulary
  • Have clear audio
  • Include a transcript
  • Are approximately one to five minutes long
Do not begin with fast films without subtitles. If almost every sentence is impossible to understand, choose easier material.
7. How to improve speaking and pronunciation

Speaking practice

  1. Listen to one short sentence.
  2. Pause the recording.
  3. Repeat the sentence aloud.
  4. Copy the speaker’s rhythm and stress.
  5. Record yourself.
  6. Compare your recording with the original.

This technique is sometimes called shadowing.

Important sounds for Arabic-speaking learners

Sounds Examples Practice
P and B park / bark Put paper near your mouth. P creates air.
V and F van / fan Your voice vibrates when saying V.
TH sounds think / this Place the tongue lightly between the teeth.
Final consonants book, hand, helped Do not remove the final sound.
Consonant groups street, school, asked Practise slowly, then increase speed.
The goal is understandable speech—not copying one particular British or American accent.
8. How to improve reading
  1. Choose a short A1 text.
  2. Read once without using a dictionary.
  3. Identify the main topic.
  4. Read again and underline unfamiliar words.
  5. Look up only the most important words.
  6. Write a one-sentence summary.

Good beginner materials

  • Signs and notices
  • Menus
  • Timetables
  • Short messages
  • Simple emails
  • Very short stories
  • Beginner dialogues
Do not translate every word. First try to understand the general meaning from the complete sentence.
9. How to improve writing

Begin with five-sentence paragraphs

My name is Ahmed. I am sixteen years old. I live in Duhok. I am a secondary-school student. I enjoy mathematics and football.

Beginner writing topics

  • Introduce yourself
  • Describe your family
  • Describe your room
  • Explain your daily routine
  • Write about your school
  • Describe what you did yesterday
  • Write your plan for tomorrow
  • Write a short message or email

Writing checklist

  • Does every sentence begin with a capital letter?
  • Does every sentence end with punctuation?
  • Does the sentence contain a subject and verb?
  • Did you check the spelling?
  • Is the verb tense correct?

A practical guide to high-school exchanges, bachelor's funding, master's and PhD opportunities, eligibility rules, documents, deadlines and the U.S. student-visa process.

This researched guide covers the major nationwide, Iraq-specific and university awards that an Iraqi applicant can realistically pursue, plus official search tools for finding additional awards. Choose your route

Start with your current education level. The application route is different for each group.

🏫 Secondary school Short, funded exchanges such as IYLEP, with current country checks. 🎓 Bachelor's / first-year Need-based aid, full rides, full tuition and automatic merit. 📚 Master's / professional Fulbright, Rotary, Humphrey, P.E.O. and university funding. 🔬 PhD / research Funded admission packages, assistantships and fellowships.

Understand the funding words

Full demonstrated need The college calculates what your family can pay, then covers the rest. It does not automatically mean zero family payment.
Full cost / full ride Usually tuition plus living costs. Flights, insurance, books or personal costs may still be excluded.
Full tuition Pays tuition only. Housing, food, insurance, travel and fees can remain expensive.
Partial scholarship A discount. You must prove that you can fund the unpaid balance before receiving Form I-20.
Funded exchange A short, non-degree programme that may cover travel, housing, meals, visa and programme costs.
Eligible to apply does not mean guaranteed funding. The most generous universities are extremely selective. Build a balanced list that includes several automatic or predictable merit options, and calculate the final amount your family must pay.

Bachelor's funding

Most U.S. colleges give little or no aid to international students. The strongest plan combines two kinds of institution: colleges that meet full demonstrated financial need and universities with large merit scholarships.

The universities below state that an international first-year applicant's request for aid does not hurt the admission decision and that they meet the institution's calculation of demonstrated need. Transfer rules can be different. Admission remains exceptionally competitive.

Automatic merit

The top GPA award is limited and offered in admission order. SAT/ACT awards can reach $20,160 per year; the higher automatic award applies rather than stacking both.

Requirements: Admitted international undergraduate meeting the university's English requirement and qualifying GPA or official score.

Apply: Automatic awards need no separate form. The separate competitive scholarship application is due 10 January; verify the entry-year table before relying on an amount.

Many full-ride merit awards above are first-year only, and international transfer aid is much rarer. Check the university's transfer definition before paying an application fee.

Master's, PhD and professional opportunities

Graduate funding is often tied to a specific degree or department. Apply to the fellowship and the university programme separately whenever the instructions require both.

🤝 Volunteering: From Interest to Real Impact

A complete starter guide for Iraqi students: what volunteering means, why it matters, how to choose a role, where to look, how to stay safe and how to turn service into evidence of genuine impact.

The main rule: volunteering is service, not a method for collecting empty certificates. A strong experience solves a real need, respects the people being served, stays within the volunteer's competence and produces honest evidence of what was done. It may help a student develop skills or support a future application, but it never guarantees admission, a scholarship or a job.

1. What does volunteering mean?

Volunteering means freely giving time, effort or a useful skill to help other people, a community, a public cause or research—without being forced and without expecting a normal wage. It can happen through an organization or directly in the community, in person or online, regularly or as a short project.

Common forms

  • Formal: a defined role in a school, charity, university club, hospital or humanitarian organization.
  • Informal: direct community help, such as a free study group or neighbourhood clean-up.
  • Skills-based: translation, design, coding, tutoring, data work or professional support.
  • Micro-volunteering: small online tasks that can be completed in short sessions.
  • Project-based: a team works toward one measurable result and then closes or hands over the project.

What it is not

  • It is not unpaid work imposed by an employer.
  • It is not performing clinical, legal or technical duties without training or authorization.
  • It is not posting photographs of vulnerable people to improve a personal profile.
  • It is not paying a large fee for a “guaranteed certificate” or an unverified trip.
  • It is not inventing hours, responsibilities, beneficiaries or impact.

Benefits when the role is a good match

🌱 Community benefit

Useful time and skills can expand services, solve small local problems and help communities organize around shared needs.

🧰 Practical skills

Communication, teamwork, planning, teaching, digital tools, problem-solving and responsibility are learned through real tasks.

🧭 Career exploration

A supervised role lets a student test an area of interest before committing to a degree or career path.

🤝 Relationships

Students can meet mentors and peers, understand different communities and reduce social isolation.

📁 Evidence for applications

Consistent service can provide honest examples of initiative, leadership and reflection. Quality and impact matter more than a long list of hours.

🧠 Wellbeing and purpose

UN Volunteers reports benefits including skills, wellbeing and reduced isolation. Research is mostly associative, so volunteering should not be presented as a guaranteed health treatment.

Choose sustainability over intensity. Two reliable hours each week for three months usually create more value than one exhausting event followed by no further involvement.

4. Your eight-week volunteer starter plan

This plan is designed for a student with about two to four hours a week. Increase the commitment only after confirming that it is safe, useful and compatible with study.

  1. Week 1 — Define your fit
    Write down three interests, three useful skills, one skill you want to learn, your age, transport limits, languages and the exact hours you can give. Choose local, online or hybrid work.
  2. Week 2 — Shortlist and verify
    Find three organizations or platforms. Read the role, eligibility, safety and time requirements. Check official contact details and remove anything that is unsafe, unaffordable or unclear.
  3. Week 3 — Contact or apply
    Send a short, specific message to each suitable organization. Explain what you can offer, what you want to learn and your actual availability. Do not send the same vague message to everyone.
  4. Week 4 — Agree the role
    Meet the supervisor, confirm tasks and boundaries, complete any training and agree on one small first-month goal. Ask how hours and outputs will be verified.
  5. Week 5 — Begin small
    Attend on time, observe how the organization works, ask questions and complete the assigned task accurately. Record facts in your private volunteer log; never record confidential client details.
  6. Week 6 — Contribute consistently
    Repeat the agreed work and request one piece of feedback. If a task is outside your skills, unsafe or unethical, pause and speak with the supervisor instead of improvising.
  7. Week 7 — Measure and improve
    Compare progress with the agreed goal. Count outputs and outcomes honestly, identify what did not work and adjust the next session.
  8. Week 8 — Review and decide
    Thank the team, request verification of your role and summarize what you learned. Decide whether to continue for three months, move to another role or hand over the work cleanly.

5. Create your own small project when no role exists

A self-led project can be valuable, but only when it begins with a real community request and has an accountable adult or institution. Do not create a “solution” before asking the people affected what they need.

  1. Listen first. Speak with a school, neighbourhood group, clinic, library or community organization. Define one specific unmet need.
  2. Find a partner. Ask a teacher, university department, licensed organization or responsible professional to supervise and approve the activity.
  3. Set one SMART goal. Make it specific, measurable, achievable, relevant and time-bound.
  4. Plan safety and resources. List permissions, location, transport, materials, privacy, first aid, weather and what happens if something goes wrong.
  5. Pilot on a small scale. Test one lesson, one event or one week before expanding. Collect feedback and correct problems.
  6. Measure, report and hand over. Compare results with the goal, report honestly, store no unnecessary personal data and leave instructions so useful work can continue.

Weak goal

“Raise awareness about study skills.”

It does not define the audience, action, result or deadline.

Stronger goal

“With the school counsellor, deliver four 45-minute study-skills sessions to 20 Grade 10 students during October and improve the average five-question quiz score by at least 20%.”

Low-cost project examples

  • Four-session reading or conversation club
  • Peer study-skills workshop with a teacher
  • Accessible digital guide for a local service
  • School waste audit followed by a realistic action
  • Humanitarian mapathon with official training
  • Translation or subtitles reviewed by a fluent supervisor
  • First-aid awareness event led by a qualified trainer
  • Beginner sports, art or music session through a community centre
Do not independently collect donations or sensitive personal information. Money, medicines, beneficiary lists and private records require a verified organization, transparent accounting, consent and secure handling.

6. Safety, ethics and boundaries

Under 18

Guardian and adult supervision

Use an established organization, obtain parent/guardian permission and confirm who supervises every activity. Do not meet unknown adults alone, enter private homes alone or use personal transport arrangements that your guardian has not approved.

Health and professional limits

Work only within competence

A student may support education, logistics or navigation under supervision, but must not act as a doctor, nurse, lawyer, counsellor, engineer or interpreter beyond verified training and authorization.

Privacy

Protect people and data

Do not place names, diagnoses, phone numbers, addresses, documents or photographs in a personal portfolio. Collect only the minimum data the organization authorizes and use its secure system.

Dignity and consent

People are not content

Explain how a photo or story will be used and obtain informed permission. A person should be able to refuse without losing help. Extra safeguards apply to children, patients and vulnerable adults.

Personal safety

Know the plan

Confirm the location, hours, responsible contact, transport, emergency procedure, required clothing or protective equipment and whether the activity involves heat, traffic, animals, debris or crowds.

Sustainable service

Protect study and health

Set a weekly limit, keep rest time and communicate early if exams or illness affect attendance. A responsible handover is better than disappearing or continuing until burnout.

Questions to ask before saying yes

  • Who supervises me and how can I contact them?
  • What exact tasks may and may not I perform?
  • Who benefits, and how was the need identified?
  • What training, ID or background checks are required?
  • Are transport, meals, equipment or expenses provided?
  • What privacy and photography rules apply?
  • How do I report misconduct, injury or safeguarding concerns?
  • How are my hours, outputs and feedback recorded?
Leave and seek help if: someone asks you to hide an incident, work without safe equipment, perform a restricted task, meet privately in an unsafe place, surrender original documents, pay an unexplained fee, misuse donations, falsify hours or share private information. Tell a trusted adult or the organization's safeguarding/complaints contact when appropriate.

7. Document your work honestly

A certificate confirms participation only. Strong evidence also shows the role, consistency, output, outcome, feedback and what you learned. Never publish confidential beneficiary information.

1. Private activity log

Record the date, hours, task, approved output, challenge, feedback and next action after every session.

2. Work samples

Keep non-confidential designs, lesson plans, code, maps, translations or reports only when the organization permits it.

3. Measured results

Use accurate numbers such as sessions delivered, pages translated, records transcribed or tasks completed—plus outcomes where they can be measured.

4. Independent verification

Keep the supervisor's name, organizational email and a signed letter or platform record describing the dates and duties.

🌍 United World Colleges — UWC

Q1: What is UWC?

United World Colleges (UWC) is a global educational movement. It brings together students from different countries, cultures and backgrounds.

🇬🇧 UK University Scholarships Through UCAS

A beginner-friendly guide for Iraqi students applying to undergraduate universities in the United Kingdom.

Q1: What is UCAS?

UCAS stands for the Universities and Colleges Admissions Service. It is the main system used to apply for undergraduate courses at UK universities.

For 2027 entry, one UCAS application allows a student to select up to five university or course choices.

UCAS normally does not award the scholarship. Scholarships are usually provided by:

  • The university itself
  • A university department
  • The UK government
  • A charity or external organisation
  • A student’s national government or sponsor

Q2: Can an Iraqi student use UCAS?

Yes. Iraqi students can apply through UCAS as international applicants.

However, every university decides which Iraqi qualifications it accepts. Having an Iraqi secondary certificate does not guarantee direct entry into the first year of every UK university.

Some universities may require:

  • An International Foundation Year
  • A-level qualifications
  • The International Baccalaureate Diploma
  • Another recognised international qualification
  • Evidence of English-language ability

For example, the University of Exeter currently states that the Iraqi National High School Diploma is not sufficient for direct undergraduate entry at Exeter. A recognised foundation programme may be required. Other universities can have different rules.

Q3: What types of scholarships are available?

  1. Automatic scholarships: the university assesses the student automatically after receiving the course application.
  2. Competitive merit scholarships: based on excellent grades, activities, achievements and the quality of the application.
  3. Need-based scholarships: based partly or entirely on the student’s financial circumstances.
  4. Country or regional scholarships: available only to applicants from certain countries or regions, such as Iraq or the Middle East.
  5. Subject scholarships: available only for particular courses such as engineering, science, business, music or sport.
  6. Tuition-fee discounts: reduce the amount paid to the university but do not provide living expenses.
  7. Full scholarships: may cover all tuition and sometimes living costs. These are uncommon and extremely competitive at undergraduate level.

1. Where should a complete beginner start?

Your first target: A1 English

English ability is commonly divided into six CEFR levels:

  1. A1: Beginner
  2. A2: Elementary
  3. B1: Intermediate
  4. B2: Upper-intermediate
  5. C1: Advanced
  6. C2: Highly proficient

At the end of A1, a student should be able to:

  • Introduce themselves
  • Say where they live and study
  • Talk simply about family and daily life
  • Ask and answer basic questions
  • Understand slow, clear everyday speech
  • Read short messages and signs
  • Write short sentences and simple messages
Important: A 12-week plan is not a promise of fluency. Progress depends on study time, practice, previous knowledge and consistency.
2. The five rules for learning English
  1. Practise regularly: 30 minutes every day is generally better than one very long lesson each week.
  2. Learn complete phrases: learn “How are you?” rather than memorising only the separate words.
  3. Use the language: listening and reading are not enough. Speak and write too.
  4. Review repeatedly: revisit vocabulary after one day, three days, one week and two weeks.
  5. Accept mistakes: mistakes show what needs more practice.
Do not only learn: “breakfast”
Learn a useful sentence: “I eat breakfast at seven o’clock.”

6. How to improve listening

Use the three-listen method

  1. First listen: listen without reading. Try to understand the main idea.
  2. Second listen: listen while reading the transcript or subtitles.
  3. Third listen: pause after every sentence and repeat it aloud.

Choose short recordings that:

  • Use beginner vocabulary
  • Have clear audio
  • Include a transcript
  • Are approximately one to five minutes long
Do not begin with fast films without subtitles. If almost every sentence is impossible to understand, choose easier material.
7. How to improve speaking and pronunciation

Speaking practice

  1. Listen to one short sentence.
  2. Pause the recording.
  3. Repeat the sentence aloud.
  4. Copy the speaker’s rhythm and stress.
  5. Record yourself.
  6. Compare your recording with the original.

This technique is sometimes called shadowing.

Important sounds for Arabic-speaking learners

Sounds Examples Practice
P and B park / bark Put paper near your mouth. P creates air.
V and F van / fan Your voice vibrates when saying V.
TH sounds think / this Place the tongue lightly between the teeth.
Final consonants book, hand, helped Do not remove the final sound.
Consonant groups street, school, asked Practise slowly, then increase speed.
The goal is understandable speech—not copying one particular British or American accent.
8. How to improve reading
  1. Choose a short A1 text.
  2. Read once without using a dictionary.
  3. Identify the main topic.
  4. Read again and underline unfamiliar words.
  5. Look up only the most important words.
  6. Write a one-sentence summary.

Good beginner materials

  • Signs and notices
  • Menus
  • Timetables
  • Short messages
  • Simple emails
  • Very short stories
  • Beginner dialogues
Do not translate every word. First try to understand the general meaning from the complete sentence.
9. How to improve writing

Begin with five-sentence paragraphs

My name is Ahmed. I am sixteen years old. I live in Duhok. I am a secondary-school student. I enjoy mathematics and football.

Beginner writing topics

  • Introduce yourself
  • Describe your family
  • Describe your room
  • Explain your daily routine
  • Write about your school
  • Describe what you did yesterday
  • Write your plan for tomorrow
  • Write a short message or email

Writing checklist

  • Does every sentence begin with a capital letter?
  • Does every sentence end with punctuation?
  • Does the sentence contain a subject and verb?
  • Did you check the spelling?
  • Is the verb tense correct?
